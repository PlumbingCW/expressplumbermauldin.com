Electronic Water Leak Detection: Tools, Limits and When It Pays Off

By the Mauldin Express Plumbing team · Published October 24, 2026 · Updated October 24, 2026

Electronic leak detection finds a hidden water leak by sound, gas, heat or pressure instead of by cutting holes. For a Mauldin, SC home, it is usually worth paying for when the water bill is climbing and nothing visible is wet.

☎ Call (855) 575-2890

The short answer before the gadgets

A plumber does not find most hidden leaks by guessing where to dig. The job starts by narrowing the leak to a zone, then uses a tool matched to the pipe material and the location. Four methods cover nearly every case: acoustic listening, tracer gas, thermal imaging and pressure testing.

Each one answers a different question. Pressure testing says whether a leak exists on a run of pipe. Listening and gas say where. Thermal imaging says which part of a wall or floor carries warm water. The best visits combine two, because any single method can mislead.

If you are weighing a call, the rule of thumb is simple. A wet spot you can see is a repair job. A bill that jumped with no visible cause, or running water you hear with every tap closed, is a job for professional leak detection. Most Mauldin homeowners land in the second group.

Four methods side by side

Here is what each method detects, where it works best and where it struggles.

Acoustic listening, in plain terms

A leaking pressurized pipe makes a faint hiss, or a low rumble if the leak is large. A ground microphone or a contact sensor on a valve picks it up, and a handheld amplifier lets the plumber hear it through headphones.

The technician walks the suspected line and listens at intervals. The sound is loudest directly above the break, so the readings form a map that narrows to a few feet. It works well on copper and galvanized steel. Plastic pipe such as PEX and PVC dampens the sound, which is why a second method often backs it up.

Tracer gas when the sound is not enough

Tracer gas is a harmless mix of about 5 percent hydrogen and 95 percent nitrogen. The plumber isolates a section of pipe, drains it, then fills it with the gas. Hydrogen is the lightest molecule there is, so it rises quickly through soil, slab and drywall.

A sniffer probe at the surface reads the gas, and the highest reading sits above the leak. It is the method of choice when the leak is too quiet to hear or the pipe is plastic. The cost is time, because the line must be drained first.

Thermal imaging and what it can and cannot show

An infrared camera turns surface temperatures into a color picture. A hot-water line leaking inside a wall or under a floor warms the surface above it, and the camera sees the warm patch. Cold-water leaks show as cooler, damp areas that dry slower than the surrounding material.

The camera sees the surface only. It cannot see through a wall to a pipe, and it reads a damp towel or a sunny window as readily as a leak. A good operator uses the image to choose where to listen or test next, not to cut a hole on its own.

How a detection visit usually runs

The order matters, because each stage rules something out cheaply before the next one starts.

  1. 1

    Confirm the leak is real

    Close every fixture, read the meter, wait 15 minutes, then read it again. If it moved, the leak is real and the visit goes ahead.

  2. 2

    Split the system

    Shut the house cutoff. If the meter still moves, the leak is outside before the house. If it stops, the leak is inside.

  3. 3

    Pressure test the section

    The plumber isolates the cold side, hot side or the yard line and watches a gauge for a drop.

  4. 4

    Pinpoint with listening, gas or heat

    The tool is chosen by pipe material and location. Marks go on the floor or ground at the strongest reading.

  5. 5

    Open the smallest hole that works

    Repair starts at the mark, not across the whole room. A good pinpoint can mean a hole the size of a dinner plate.

What Greenville Water's meter rule means for Mauldin owners

Greenville Water maintains the line up to the outlet side of the meter. According to its customer service page, the line leaving the meter is the customer's service line and the customer's responsibility. So a leak between the meter and your house is yours to find and fix, even though the pipe sits in the yard.

That makes the meter the first free detection tool you own. Greenville Water's check is to read the meter, wait at least 15 minutes with no water in use, and see if it moved. If you shut the house cutoff and an outdoor meter still runs, the leak is on the buried stretch, and a plumber brings listening gear to the yard.

One more local detail helps avoid false alarms. Greenville Water treats its supply with chloramine, a chlorine and ammonia blend. A worn toilet flapper can leak silently into the bowl, so run Greenville Water's dye test first: food coloring in the tank, no flush, and a look at the bowl after an hour.

Signs electronic detection is worth paying for

Any one of these is a reason to call. Two or more is a strong case.

  • ✓The meter moves with every fixture closed.
  • ✓A water bill climbs with no change in how many people live in the home.
  • ✓You hear running or hissing water in a quiet house.
  • ✓A floor feels warm in one spot, with no radiant heat installed.
  • ✓A wall or ceiling stain keeps coming back after a patch.
  • ✓Water pressure drops without a clear cause.

If you see actively gushing water, skip the diagnosis. Shut off the main and call the 24/7 line.

What to do yourself and what to hand off

Homeowners can handle the meter check, the toilet dye test, the spigot check and a quiet walk-through. Those cost nothing and clear many calls. Photograph the meter dial before and after so the result is clear.

Tracer gas, a ground microphone or a pressure rig is a plumber's job, and so is opening a slab or wall. A wrong cut costs more than the detection fee. When the leak sits on the yard line, a plumber can explain repairing the buried water service line once it is located.

Questions to ask before you book

Ask which methods the plumber plans to use and why. A thermal camera alone for a buried line is a red flag. Ask whether the price covers pinpointing only or the repair too.

Ask how the quote changes if the first method finds nothing. The same kinds of hidden leaks turn up in Greenville and Greer, so a good answer sounds the same everywhere.
