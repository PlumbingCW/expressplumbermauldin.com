What to Know Before Your Shower Plumbing Rough-In

By the Mauldin Express Plumbing team · Published October 24, 2026 · Updated October 24, 2026

A shower rough-in is the plumbing that gets built inside the wall and floor before any tile or surround goes up: the drain, the vent, the supply lines and the valve body. For a Mauldin, SC remodel, this is the stage where a wrong measurement costs an hour to fix. After the tile is set, the same mistake costs a demolition.

What a rough-in covers and what it does not

Rough-in means everything a plumber installs before the finish work, the kind of job covered under new plumbing installation. That is the 2-inch drain and trap, the vent that lets the drain breathe, the half-inch hot and cold supply lines, and the mixing valve body set at the right depth in the wall. It does not include the trim kit, the showerhead, the tile or the glass.

Think of it as the skeleton. Once the wall is covered, you can only reach it by cutting into it. That is why most of your decisions about a shower, including where the drain sits and how high the head hangs, are really made at this stage.

Measurements to confirm before the walls close

These are common targets, not a substitute for your fixture's installation sheet. Valve and drain manufacturers publish exact depths, so keep the paperwork for your plumber.

ItemTypical targetWhy it matters
Drain and trap2 inches, with a P-trapMost plumbing codes call for 2 inches on a shower. A smaller pipe backs up faster.
Drain slope1/4 inch per footToo flat and the line holds water and debris. Too steep can leave solids behind.
Valve heightOften 38 to 48 inches off the floorYou should be able to start the water without standing in the spray.
Showerhead armRoughly 72 to 84 inchesTall users and rain heads need the high end of the range.
Supply pipe1/2 inch hot and coldUndersized lines starve a multi-head setup.
BlockingSolid wood or metal behind the valve and any grab barScrews need something solid to bite into years from now.

Check your valve manufacturer's rough-in depth. Tile thickness changes where the trim needs to sit.

How a rough-in usually runs, start to finish

  1. 1

    Open the wall and floor

    Demo exposes the old drain, the studs and the supply lines. This is when hidden rot or old leaks show up.

  2. 2

    Set the drain and vent

    The plumber sets the trap and connects it to the branch line with the right slope. On a slab, that can mean cutting concrete.

  3. 3

    Run supplies and mount the valve

    Hot and cold lines tie into the valve body, which is fixed to blocking at the depth the manufacturer lists.

  4. 4

    Pressure test the lines

    Caps go on, the lines are filled with air or water, and a gauge should hold steady. A slow drop means a bad joint.

  5. 5

    Schedule the inspection

    Where a permit applies, the inspector looks at the rough-in while it is still open.

  6. 6

    Flood test the pan

    Before tile, the shower pan or liner is filled and left for a day. A dropping water level means a leak that tile would have hidden.

Permits and codes for a shower rough-in in Mauldin

The City of Mauldin Building and Zoning office says a permit is required before you add, alter or replace any plumbing system. Moving a shower drain or adding a valve counts as altering plumbing. The city also says contractors must be licensed, bonded and insured and must have the permit in hand before starting.

On codes, South Carolina adopted the 2021 SC Plumbing Code, or the 2021 International Plumbing Code with state modifications, and local enforcement has been required since January 1, 2023. The 2024 SC codes are scheduled to take effect January 1, 2027. If your project may run across that date, ask the Mauldin Building and Zoning office which edition applies to your permit.

Why the drain location drives the budget

Keeping the shower where the old one was is the cheap path, because the drain and vent are already there. Moving it a few feet in a house with a crawlspace is usually manageable. Moving it on a concrete slab means cutting the floor, trenching, and patching.

Typical U.S. plumbing-only costs for a rough-in run from about $800 to $3,000, and a slab relocation can push past that. The real number depends on access, the distance to the branch line and what the open wall reveals. A call and a look at the space is the only way to get a firm figure.

Before you call a plumber for a rough-in

A short list of decisions makes the visit faster and the quote clearer.

  • ✓Pick the fixture first, since valve depth and drain size come from its paperwork.
  • ✓Decide on the showerhead style, including any rain head or handheld.
  • ✓Note whether the floor is a slab, a crawlspace or an upper level.
  • ✓Photograph the existing drain location and nearby walls.
  • ✓Ask whether your tile or surround needs extra wall depth for the valve.
  • ✓Confirm who will pull the permit and schedule the inspection.

The flood test most people skip

A pan that leaks slowly will not show anything for months. Then a stain appears on the ceiling below, or the subfloor softens. A 24-hour flood test costs a few gallons of water and one day. If you need to trace a leak later, a plumber can use leak detection equipment, but finding it before tile is far cheaper.

DIY or plumber: where the line is

Swapping a showerhead, trim kit or cartridge is a fair DIY job. Soldering or crimping new supply lines, setting a drain at the right slope and passing an inspection is not, especially because Mauldin requires a licensed, bonded and insured contractor to pull the permit.

If you are determined to do the carpentry, such as framing and blocking, a plumber can still do the rough-in in a short visit. Splitting the work this way is a fair request, and the same dispatch line serves Simpsonville and Greenville as well as Mauldin.
