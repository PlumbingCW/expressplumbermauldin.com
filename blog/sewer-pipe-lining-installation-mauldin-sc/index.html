Cured-in-Place Sewer Lining: Install Steps, Limits and the Dig Alternative

By the Mauldin Express Plumbing team · Published October 24, 2026 · Updated October 24, 2026

Cured-in-place lining installs a new resin pipe inside your old sewer pipe, so there is no trench across the yard. In Mauldin, SC it suits cracked or root-damaged pipe that still holds its round shape, and it does not suit a collapsed or sagging line.

What a liner actually is

A liner is a flexible felt tube soaked in epoxy resin. Once it sits inside the old pipe and the resin hardens, the felt becomes a rigid pipe within a pipe. It bridges cracks, seals joints and blocks roots that were squeezing through gaps.

The finished liner is smooth and has no joints, which is why a lined pipe tends to clear easier afterward. It is a repair of the pipe wall, not a replacement of the pipe route. That distinction decides almost every question that follows. If the route is wrong, a liner repeats the wrong route.

How the installation runs, start to finish

Details vary by crew and by pipe, but the sequence is consistent.

  1. 1

    Camera survey first

    A sewer camera shows the cracks, offsets, roots, belly and pipe material. The plumber also locates the line so the access points are chosen with real data.

  2. 2

    Clean the pipe

    Hydro jetting or mechanical cutting removes roots, scale and debris. Resin will not bond to a dirty wall, so the pipe has to be bare.

  3. 3

    Measure and cut the liner

    The felt tube is cut to the length and diameter of the run, then saturated with resin so it is evenly wet.

  4. 4

    Pull or invert it into place

    Air or water pressure pushes the liner through the pipe, turning it inside out so the resin side presses against the old wall.

  5. 5

    Cure and reopen

    Steam, hot water or UV light hardens the resin. A cutter reopens side connections, and a final camera pass checks the result.

When a liner is the right call

Lining fits when the pipe is intact but aging. Common examples are hairline cracks, joints pulling apart, root intrusion that returns every year, and old clay or cast iron that is scaled but still round. It also makes sense when the pipe runs under a driveway, a patio or mature trees.

The savings are real when digging would mean cutting concrete and replanting a yard. The camera survey is what proves it, so ask to see the footage before you agree to anything. A plumber who recommends lining without a camera pass is guessing.

When a liner will not work

A collapsed pipe cannot take a liner because the tube cannot pass through. A belly, which is a low spot that holds water, stays a belly after lining and keeps collecting waste. A severely offset joint can snag the liner. Pipe wrapped in heavy corrosion may need to be cleaned or replaced before anything goes in.

In those cases digging, or a pipe bursting method that pulls new pipe along the old route, is the honest answer. Paying for a liner on a failed pipe wastes money and often fails again within a season.

Who owns the sewer pipe on a Mauldin lot

The City of Mauldin Public Works Committee agenda from October 2024 describes the city sewer system as consisting only of the mainlines and manholes within street rights-of-way and utility easements. The lateral, the pipe from your house to that mainline, belongs to and is maintained by the property owner.

In practice, that means a lining or digging job on your lateral is your decision and your cost. It also means you should confirm exactly where your property ends and the city mainline begins before authorizing work. Call Public Works at (864) 289-8904 with your address to ask.

Permit rules matter too. The City of Mauldin Building and Zoning page says a permit is required before you add, alter, or replace any plumbing system, and that contractors must be licensed, bonded and insured and hold the permit before starting. Whether a liner counts as altering the system is a question for that office at (864) 234-3475.

Pipe material under a Mauldin lot

Older laterals were often clay or cast iron, and newer ones are PVC. You cannot tell by looking at the house. A camera inspection settles it, and it should happen before anyone prices a liner. If you are buying a house, a sewer camera inspection before closing is cheaper than finding out afterward.

Questions to ask a plumber before you approve lining

  • ✓Can I see the camera footage of my own pipe?
  • ✓What is the pipe material and diameter, and how long is the run?
  • ✓Is there a belly or an offset that a liner cannot fix?
  • ✓How will the side connections be reopened and checked?
  • ✓Is the permit being pulled, and by whom?
  • ✓What is the written scope, and what happens if the liner will not pass?

Typical lining costs run from a few thousand dollars for a short run to well above that for long or deep lines, and trenching has its own range. The real figure depends on length, depth, access and pipe condition, and comes from a call.

Signs your sewer line needs a look

Watch for gurgling toilets, slow drains in more than one fixture, a sewage smell in the yard, or a green, soggy patch above the line. A single slow sink is usually a local clog. Several fixtures backing up at once points to the main.

If sewage is coming up in a shower or tub, stop using water in the house and call. A backup is a health problem. A plumber can clear the line first, then use a camera to see whether repair is needed or cleaning is enough.

DIY, lining kits and where to stop

Homeowners can run a snake into a cleanout or check where the cleanout sits. That is about the limit. Lining needs a camera, a jetter, resin handling and curing equipment, and a mistake means a blocked line with a hardened tube inside it.

Do not pour a root killer down the toilet and call it a repair. It may slow roots for a while but it does not close the crack. If you want another look at the options, Fountain Inn and Simpsonville homeowners ask the same questions, and the answers do not change by town.
